LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Identify the forces acting on a simple pendulum and describe how the restoring force
depends on the displacement angle. Determine the period of a simple pendulum and explain
how it is dictated by the length of the string and the acceleration due to gravity.
SCENE PLAN
1. Hook — Have you ever wondered why grandfather clocks keep such precise time
regardless of how heavy their swinging weights are? The secret lies in the physics of a
simple pendulum, where the time it takes to complete one swing depends only on its
length and gravity.
2. Construction — Draw a fixed pivot point at the top of the screen and suspend a string
of length L from it. Attach a spherical pendulum bob of mass m to the bottom of the
string, and draw a dashed vertical line down from the pivot to represent the equilibrium
position. Displace the bob to the right by an angle theta, drawing the arc length s, the
downward weight vector mg, the tension vector along the string, and the restoring force
vector -mg sin(theta) pointing back toward equilibrium.
3. Demonstration — Drag the pendulum bob to change the initial angle and watch the
restoring force vector dynamically scale in magnitude. As the angle theta increases from
0 to 15 degrees, the component of gravity acting along the arc, -mg sin(theta), grows
larger, providing a stronger restoring force toward the equilibrium position.
4. Demonstration — Adjust a slider to modify the string length and observe how the
period of the pendulum's oscillation changes. Increasing the string length L from 0.5
meters to 2.0 meters causes the period T to increase, as the period is equal to 2 pi
times the square root of L over g.
5. Consolidation — The motion of a simple pendulum is driven by a restoring force from
gravity that scales with the sine of the displacement angle. For small angles, this
results in simple harmonic motion where the period is completely determined by the
length of the pendulum and the local acceleration due to gravity.
CONSTRAINTS
- Render in 2D unless the figure is inherently 3D. Keep every axis, label and symbol
legible at 720p; no gratuitous camera motion.
- Name the physical quantities and their units; keep the geometry quantitatively honest.
- Each demonstration must actually play out on screen: sweep the parameter through its
range and show the figure responding.
- Narration must be synchronised to the visual it describes.

Pendulums
Learning Objectives By the end of this section, you will be able to:
State the forces that act on a simple pendulum
Determine the angular frequency, frequency, and period of a simple pendulum in terms of
the length of the pendulum and the acceleration due to gravity
Define the period for a physical pendulum
Define the period for a torsional pendulum
Pendulums are in common usage. Grandfather clocks use a pendulum to keep time and a
pendulum can be used to measure the acceleration due to gravity. For small
displacements, a pendulum is a simple harmonic oscillator.
The Simple Pendulum
A simple pendulum is defined to have a point mass, also known as the pendulum bob ,
which is suspended from a string of length L with negligible mass ( Figure 15.20 ).
Here, the only forces acting on the bob are the force of gravity (i.e., the weight of
the bob) and tension from the string. The mass of the string is assumed to be negligible
as compared to the mass of the bob.

The period of a simple pendulum depends on its length and the acceleration due to
gravity. The period is completely independent of other factors, such as mass and the
maximum displacement. As with simple harmonic oscillators, the period T for a pendulum
is nearly independent of amplitude, especially if θ θ is less than about 15 ° . 15 ° .
Even simple pendulum clocks can be finely adjusted and remain accurate.
Note the dependence of T on g . If the length of a pendulum is precisely known, it can
actually be used to measure the acceleration due to gravity, as in the following
example.

Measuring Acceleration due to Gravity by the Period of a Pendulum
What is the acceleration due to gravity in a region where a simple pendulum having a
length 75.000 cm has a period of 1.7357 s?
Strategy
We are asked to find g given the period T and the length L of a pendulum. We can solve T
= 2 π L g T = 2 π L g for g , assuming only that the angle of deflection is less than 15
° 15 ° .
Solution
Square T = 2 π L g T = 2 π L g and solve for g :
g = 4 π 2 L T 2 . g = 4 π 2 L T 2 .
Substitute known values into the new equation:
g = 4 π 2 0.75000 m ( 1.7357 s ) 2 . g = 4 π 2 0.75000 m ( 1.7357 s ) 2 .
Calculate to find g :
g = 9.8281 m/s 2 . g = 9.8281 m/s 2 .

Physical Pendulum
Any object can oscillate like a pendulum. Consider a coffee mug hanging on a hook in the
pantry. If the mug gets knocked, it oscillates back and forth like a pendulum until the
oscillations die out. We have described a simple pendulum as a point mass and a string.
A physical pendulum is any object whose oscillations are similar to those of the simple
pendulum, but cannot be modeled as a point mass on a string, and the mass distribution
must be included into the equation of motion.
As for the simple pendulum, the restoring force of the physical pendulum is the force of
gravity. With the simple pendulum, the force of gravity acts on the center of the
pendulum bob. In the case of the physical pendulum, the force of gravity acts on the
center of mass (CM) of an object. The object oscillates about a point O . Consider an
object of a generic shape as shown in Figure 15.21 .

Torsional Pendulum
A torsional pendulum consists of a rigid body suspended by a light wire or spring (

released from rest, the body oscillates between ( θ = + Θ ) ( θ = + Θ ) and ( θ = − Θ )
( θ = − Θ ) . The restoring torque is supplied by the shearing of the string or wire.
